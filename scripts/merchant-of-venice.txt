Let's do The Merchant of Venice in two minutes.
Bassanio is a young man in Venice with expensive taste and no money. He wants to woo Portia, a rich heiress in a place called Belmont, and he needs cash to look the part. So he asks his friend Antonio, a merchant, for a loan.
Antonio can't help. All his money is tied up in ships, and the ships are at sea. So they go to a moneylender called Shylock.
Shylock has history with Antonio. Antonio has spent years insulting him in public, because of who he is, and lending money without charging interest, which undercuts his business. Shylock agrees to the loan, with one unusual condition: if it isn't repaid on time, he gets a pound of Antonio's flesh. Antonio says that's fine. It's a joke, he thinks.
Meanwhile, in Belmont, Portia's late father has left a strange will. Every suitor must choose between three caskets, gold, silver and lead. Pick the right one and you marry her. Bassanio picks lead, which is right, and the two are engaged.
Then the bad news arrives. Antonio's ships are lost, and the loan is due. Shylock wants his pound of flesh, and he takes it to court.
Portia arrives disguised as a young lawyer. She tells the court the contract is valid. Then she points out the fine print: it says a pound of flesh, but not one drop of blood. Shylock can't take one without the other. He loses the case, and the court takes half his wealth and forces him to convert. The play treats that as justice, and it sits very uncomfortably with modern audiences.
There's a short comic coda with rings and mistaken identities, and then it's over.
It's one of Shakespeare's most debated plays. People still argue about how to stage it, and how to treat Shylock.
The moral: read the contract.
