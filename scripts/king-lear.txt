Let's do King Lear in two minutes.
Lear is an old king who wants to retire. Before he does, he asks his three daughters to tell him how much they love him. The best answer gets the biggest slice of the kingdom.
Goneril goes first, and she gushes. Regan goes next, and she gushes harder. Then it's Cordelia, the youngest, and the one who actually means it. She says she loves him as a daughter should, no more and no less. Lear hears that as an insult. He cuts her off, banishes her, and splits her share between her sisters.
His friend Kent says this is a mistake. Lear banishes him too.
Now Lear stays with Goneril and Regan, and it goes badly. They shrink his household, shut doors on him, and soon he's out on a heath in a storm, shouting at the weather. He is, in his own words, more sinned against than sinning. That's mostly true.
Meanwhile, a second family falls apart in the same way. Gloucester has two sons. Edmund, the one who isn't trusted, forges a letter to make Gloucester think the good son, Edgar, is plotting against him. Gloucester believes it. Edgar has to go on the run in disguise. Edmund moves up, and Gloucester pays heavily for trusting the wrong child.
Cordelia returns with an army to rescue her father. She finds him again and they make peace. It doesn't last. Edmund's side wins the battle, and Cordelia is killed.
Lear carries her body in, and dies of grief.
It's one of Shakespeare's grimmest tragedies. For a long stretch of history, theatres staged a rewritten version where everyone survived, because audiences couldn't take the original.
The moral: when someone tells you the truth, don't banish them.
That was King Lear. Shake you later.
